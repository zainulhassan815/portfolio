Lynxsta: a booking that is real because it is paid for.

Role
Sole engineer, design and build
Engagement
Fixed price, milestone based
Started
March 2026, ongoing
Stack
Next.js, Supabase, Stripe Connect
Who it is for

Hairstylists, lash techs and nail artists who take bookings by WhatsApp and Instagram. A message, a price, a date, and nothing binding. For a solo operator, one no-show is that day's income gone.

What it does

Stylists build reusable service templates, then spin up a booking link per client in seconds. The client walks four short steps, appointment, prep, terms, payment, and can leave and come back without losing their place. The slot is held for 20 minutes while they pay.

What it changes

Every booking arrives paid for, on the record, with the cancellation terms already agreed. The deposit and the platform fee are taken as a single payment, so the stylist is never chasing a half-finished booking.

The brief, and where I argued with it

The spec asked for two separate charges, a platform fee and a stylist deposit. That is two independent failure points: the fee lands, the deposit does not, and someone reconciles a half-paid booking by hand. I collapsed it into one Stripe destination charge, so the deposit and the fee are captured together or not at all.

It also asked for an event-driven architecture. Standing up a message queue for an MVP at this scale solves a problem the product does not have yet, so events and state changes commit in the same Postgres transaction and drain through a cron-run outbox. Same guarantee that state and audit trail cannot drift apart, none of the operational weight, and a clean migration path to a real queue if the volume ever asks for one.

The hard part is timing, not checkout

A payment can land a second after a hold expired. A Stripe webhook and the expiry sweep can hit the same booking within moments of each other. So every state transition is a conditional update on a version column: whichever writer loses the race gets zero rows affected and backs off cleanly instead of the two of them stomping on each other.

Underneath that, a Postgres constraint allows at most one completed payment per booking, whatever the application code does. The 20 minute hold is enforced three ways rather than one, because each on its own has a hole: a client-side countdown is cosmetic, a check on next request misses anyone who never comes back, and a periodic sweep alone has latency. A 60 second grace buffer keeps a payment that is genuinely in flight from being expired out from under the client.

Shipped, then changed by real use

The MVP launched as a single confirmation step. Once the stylist was using it with real clients, the feedback was that clients needed the agreement broken into separately acknowledged steps, and needed to be able to leave and come back. I rebuilt it as a four stage flow where the payment endpoint independently verifies all four stages completed before Stripe Checkout opens, so the frontend sequencing is not the only gate. Reusable service templates came out of the same round, so the stylist stopped retyping the same service for every booking.

Where it stands

Launched on every original acceptance criterion and in daily use. Booking volume and no-show numbers to be confirmed.
